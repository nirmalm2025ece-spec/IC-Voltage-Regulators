1. Introduction and Learning Objectives

Definition: An IC voltage regulator is an integrated circuit that maintains a nearly constant DC output voltage despite changes in input voltage, load current, and temperature, within its specified operating limits.

Rectifiers and filters convert AC into an unregulated DC supply. A regulator stabilizes that DC voltage so electronic circuits receive a suitable supply.

Core idea: The regulator senses the output, compares it with an internal reference, and adjusts its pass element or switching duty cycle to reduce output-voltage error.

Learning objectives

  • Explain line and load regulation.
  • Distinguish 78xx, 79xx, LM317, LDO and switching regulators.
  • Calculate output voltage, power loss and efficiency.
  • Choose capacitors, heatsinking and protection.
  • Debug common regulator-circuit faults.

Prerequisites

  • Ohm’s law and Kirchhoff’s laws
  • Diodes, transistors and op-amp feedback
  • Rectifier, filter capacitor and DC supplies
  • Power, current, thermal resistance and datasheets

Important terms

TermMeaning
Reference voltageStable internal voltage used as the comparison standard.
Line regulationOutput change caused by input-voltage change at a fixed load.
Load regulationOutput change caused by load-current change at a fixed input.
Dropout voltageMinimum input-to-output headroom needed to regulate.
Ripple rejection / PSRRAbility to reduce input ripple or disturbances appearing at the output; definitions depend on frequency and datasheet convention.

2. Classification of IC Voltage Regulators

Linear · Fixed

78xx series

Positive fixed output regulators. Example: 7805 gives nominal +5 V; 7812 gives nominal +12 V.

Commonly used for simple, low-noise supplies when sufficient input headroom is available.

Linear · Fixed

79xx series

Negative fixed output regulators. Example: 7905 gives nominal −5 V; 7912 gives nominal −12 V.

Useful for dual-polarity op-amp supplies.

Linear · Adjustable

LM317

Adjustable positive regulator. A resistor divider between OUT, ADJ and ground sets the output voltage.

Use the exact datasheet for current, dropout and capacitor requirements.

Linear · Low dropout

LDO regulators

Designed to regulate with a relatively small input-output voltage difference.

Stability may depend strongly on output capacitance and its ESR.

Switching

Buck converter

Efficiently steps a DC voltage down using a switch, inductor, diode or synchronous switch, and capacitors.

Switching

Boost / buck-boost

Boost raises voltage; buck-boost topologies can produce output above or below input, depending on topology.

Selection reminder: “78xx” and “79xx” are family names, not a guarantee that every device has the same pinout, current rating, thermal limit or capacitor requirements. Check the exact part’s datasheet.

B. Adjustable LM317 regulator (conceptual)

Vout ≈ Vref (1 + R2/R1) + Iadj × R2

For a typical LM317, Vref is approximately 1.25 V. R1 is connected from OUT to ADJ; R2 is connected from ADJ to ground. The exact current and resistor limits are datasheet-dependent.

4. Working Principle

  1. Reference: An internal reference establishes the target voltage.
  2. Feedback sensing: A fraction of output voltage is compared with the reference.
  3. Error amplification: The control circuit detects whether output is too high or too low.
  4. Correction: A linear regulator adjusts its series pass element; a switching regulator adjusts switching behaviour.
  5. Protection: Many ICs include current limiting and thermal shutdown, but protection features vary by part.

DC behaviour

Once the input is high enough and load current and temperature remain within the specified range, the regulator maintains the nominal output. If input falls below the required headroom, the output drops out of regulation.

AC and transient behaviour

Input ripple and sudden load changes are disturbances. The regulator attenuates them according to its frequency-dependent response, output capacitor, layout and load. No regulator removes all ripple at all frequencies.

Linear-regulator power loss: Ploss ≈ (Vin − Vout) × Iout

This approximation neglects ground/quiescent current and other losses. Use a more complete datasheet-based calculation when accuracy matters.

5. Equations, Design and Solved Numericals

Essential equations

Line regulation = ΔVout / ΔVin (at fixed load)
Load regulation = change in Vout as load current changes (at fixed Vin)
Ploss ≈ (Vin − Vout) Iout
ηlinear ≈ (Vout Iout)/(Vin Iin) × 100%
LM317: Vout ≈ 1.25(1 + R2/R1) + Iadj R2

Line regulation may be specified in mV/V or as a percentage. Load regulation may be specified in mV or percentage between defined load conditions. Always follow the datasheet’s stated test conditions and sign convention.

Example 1 — 7805 power dissipation

Given: Vin = 12 V, Vout = 5 V, Iout = 0.20 A. Find idealized regulator power loss.

Formula: Ploss ≈ (Vin − Vout)Iout

Substitution: (12 − 5) × 0.20 = 7 × 0.20

Answer: 1.4 W. This is significant heat for a small package; check junction temperature and heatsink requirements.

Example 2 — LM317 output voltage

Given: R1 = 240 Ω, R2 = 720 Ω, Vref = 1.25 V. Ignore Iadj for this estimate.

Vout ≈ 1.25(1 + 720/240) = 1.25(1 + 3) = 1.25 × 4.

Answer: approximately 5.0 V. Actual output also depends on Iadj, tolerances and operating conditions.

Example 3 — GATE-style efficiency estimate

Given: Linear regulator input = 9 V, output = 5 V, output current = 0.10 A. Neglect quiescent current. Estimate efficiency and power loss.

Pout = 5 × 0.10 = 0.50 W. Pin ≈ 9 × 0.10 = 0.90 W.

η ≈ (0.50/0.90) × 100% = 55.6%. Ploss ≈ (9 − 5) × 0.10 = 0.40 W.

Answer: efficiency ≈ 55.6%; power loss ≈ 0.40 W. A switching regulator may be preferable when efficiency is important.

Practical design checklist

  • Verify minimum and maximum input voltage, including ripple and transients.
  • Confirm output voltage, maximum load current and dropout/headroom.
  • Calculate worst-case dissipation and junction temperature.
  • Use recommended input/output capacitors and observe ESR requirements.
  • Check current limit, safe operating area, thermal shutdown and reverse-current behaviour.
  • Keep high-current loops short; use suitable ground layout and decoupling.
  • Verify pinout and tab connection from the exact datasheet.

6. Linear vs Switching Regulators

FeatureLinear (e.g., 7805, LM317, LDO)Switching (buck/boost)
OperationPass element operates in its linear region.Switches energy through an inductor/capacitor network.
EfficiencyOften limited by voltage drop; ideal estimate roughly Vout/Vin.Usually higher, but depends on topology, load and design.
NoiseGenerally low switching noise.Switching ripple/EMI requires careful layout and filtering.
ComplexityUsually simpler.More components and layout considerations.
Best fitLow-noise, modest power, small Vin–Vout difference.Large conversion ratio or higher-efficiency power conversion.

7. GATE-Style Practice Questions

These are original practice questions, not claimed to be actual previous-year GATE questions. Open each item to view its answer and explanation.

Q1 · Easy — What is the nominal output of a 7805?

Answer: +5 V. The 78xx family denotes positive fixed regulators; “05” identifies the nominal 5 V output.

Q2 · Easy — Which family commonly provides fixed negative outputs?

Answer: 79xx. For example, 7905 is nominally −5 V.

Q3 · Moderate — Vin = 10 V, Vout = 5 V, Iout = 0.2 A. Approximate linear-regulator loss?

Answer: 1 W. Ploss ≈ (10 − 5) × 0.2 = 1 W.

Q4 · Moderate — A linear regulator has Vin = 10 V and Vout = 5 V. Neglecting quiescent current, what is the approximate efficiency?

Answer: 50%. η ≈ Vout/Vin × 100% = 5/10 × 100%.

Q5 · Moderate — What happens when Vin becomes too close to Vout for a conventional regulator?

Answer: It loses regulation as it enters dropout; output can fall below its target.

Q6 · Easy — What is the main purpose of feedback in a regulator?

Answer: To sense output error and adjust the control element to stabilize the output.

Q7 · Moderate — For LM317, R1 = 240 Ω and R2 = 480 Ω. Ignoring Iadj, estimate Vout.

Answer: 3.75 V. Vout ≈ 1.25(1 + 480/240) = 1.25 × 3.

Q8 · Difficult — Why can an LDO oscillate after changing its output capacitor?

Answer: The control loop may depend on output capacitance and ESR. A capacitor outside the allowed range can reduce stability margin.

Q9 · Moderate — Which regulator type is usually preferred for efficient step-down conversion from a high DC input?

Answer: Buck switching regulator. It transfers energy through switching components and can reduce voltage with lower losses than a linear regulator.

Q10 · Difficult — A 5 V linear regulator draws 0.3 A from 15 V. Estimate loss, neglecting quiescent current.

Answer: 3 W. (15 − 5) × 0.3 = 3 W. Thermal design is essential.

8. Core Electronics Interview Preparation

1. What is a voltage regulator?

A circuit that maintains a stable output voltage against specified input, load and temperature variations.

2. Explain line regulation.

It measures output-voltage variation due to input-voltage change while load is held constant. Datasheets specify test conditions and units.

3. Explain load regulation.

It measures output-voltage change between specified load conditions at a fixed input voltage.

4. Why does a 7805 become hot?

It dissipates approximately (Vin − 5 V) × load current. Reduce voltage drop/current, improve heatsinking, or consider a switching converter.

5. What is dropout voltage?

The minimum headroom between input and output required to maintain regulation. It varies with load and temperature.

6. Why use an LDO instead of a standard linear regulator?

An LDO can regulate with a smaller input-output difference, useful when supply headroom is limited. It is not automatically more efficient in every condition.

7. How do you select input and output capacitors?

Follow the exact IC datasheet for capacitance, voltage rating, ESR and placement. Consider source impedance and transient load requirements.

8. What protections might an IC regulator include?

Current limiting, thermal shutdown and sometimes safe-operating-area or short-circuit protection. Confirm the particular device specification.

9. The output is lower than expected. What do you check?

Measure input under load, confirm pinout and ground, check load current, dropout, wiring, thermal shutdown, capacitor connections and whether the part is damaged.

10. Why might a switching regulator create EMI?

Fast switching edges and pulsed currents produce high-frequency energy. Minimize hot-loop area, place input capacitors close, use a suitable layout and validate filtering.

11. What is PSRR?

Power-supply rejection ratio describes how much a supply disturbance is attenuated at the output, usually with frequency and operating conditions specified.

12. How would you test a regulator in the lab?

Use a current-limited supply and suitable load; measure output across input and load ranges; observe ripple/transients with correct probe grounding; monitor temperature and compare with datasheet limits.

High-priority points

  • 78xx: positive fixed regulator; 79xx: negative fixed regulator.
  • LM317: adjustable positive regulator.
  • Dropout occurs when the input headroom is insufficient.
  • Linear-regulator efficiency is constrained by the voltage drop and current.
  • High power loss means high junction temperature; verify thermal limits.
  • Capacitor stability requirements differ by regulator IC.
  • Switching regulators are often better for high efficiency, but require EMI-aware layout.
